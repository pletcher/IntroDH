# Introduction

## Roland Barthes, "From Work to Text"

- Originally published in _Revue d'esthétiques_ in 1971
- Hayles uses Barthes as a way into the problem of media-specific analysis (MSA)

## "From Work to Text"

> [T]he reader plays twice over: he _plays at_ the Text (ludic meaning),
> he seeks a practice which reproduces it; but, so that this practice
> is not reduced to a passive, interior _mimesis_ (the Text being precisely
> what resists this reduction), he _plays_ the Text; we must not forget
> that _play_ [_jouer_] is also a musical term; the history of music
> (as practice, not as "art") is, moreover, quite parallel to that of the
> Text... (Barthes 1986, 63–64)

# Hypertext

## Print and digital

> Hypertext, understood as a genre that can be implemented in both print and
digital media, offers an ideal opportunity to explore the dynamic interaction
between the artifactual characteristics and the interpretation that materiality
embodies. (Hayles 2004, 72)

## A means to play

> The boundary [between hypertext and everything else] is to be regarded
as heuristic, operating not as a rigid barrier but as a borderland inviting
playful forays that test the limits of the form by modifying, enlarging,
or transforming them. (Hayles 2004, 72–73)

# 9 Points

## 1. Electronic hypertexts are dynamic images

> Code always has some layers that remain invisible and inaccessible to
most users. From this we arrive at an obvious but nevertheless central
maxim: print is flat, code is deep. (Hayles 2004, 75)

::: notes

Hayles is making a narrower argument than it seems here. Pre-print
books also had invisible layers — of labor, access, transmission — that
are not accessible to most "users."

:::

## 4. Electronic hypertexts have depth and operate in three dimensions

- Espen Aarseth's (1997) _scripton_ (surface image) versus _texton_
  (underlying code) (Hayles 2004, 78)

## 4. Electronic hypertexts have depth and operate in three dimensions

> Scriptons always include the screen image but can also include any code
visible to a user who is able to access different layers of program. Textons
can appear in print as well as electronic media. Stipple engraving, although
it is normally perceived by the reader as a continuous image, operates through
the binary digital distinction of ink dot/no ink dot; here the scripton is
the image and the ink dots are the textons. In electronic media, textons
and scriptons operate in a vertical hierarchy rather than through the flat
microscale/macroscale play of stipple engraving. (Hayles 2004, 78)

# Reading as a cyborg

## The end of books?

> Digital media have given us an opportunity we have not had for the last
several hundred years: the chance to see print with new eyes and, with that
chance, the possibility of understanding how deeply literary theory and
criticism have been imbued with assumptions specific to print. (Hayles, 2004,
